# The Genericity Test

The Genericity Test is Employer Brand Auditor's signature diagnostic. It answers one question about a claim: **could this have been written by almost anyone?** A generic claim costs space, attention and credibility while giving the candidate no reason to choose this employer.

Apply it to central claims: the EVP line, pillar headlines, and claims repeated across touchpoints. In a full audit, test 3–6 claims. In an EVP stress test, test the proposition and every pillar. In a content review, test the 2–4 claims that carry the asset.

---

## 1. The four tests

### Test 1 — COMPETITOR SUBSTITUTION
*Could a comparable employer plausibly use the same claim unchanged?*

Method: replace the employer's name with "a comparable employer" (same sector, similar roles, same labour market) and read the claim again.
- **Breaks** — the claim becomes false or nonsensical because it depends on something specific (a place, a number, a named practice, a constraint, a customer).
- **Bends** — the claim mostly survives, but one specific element would need changing.
- **Survives** — the claim reads equally true for the substitute.

This is a thought experiment about the wording. Phrase the result as "plausibly reusable by a comparable employer because it contains no employer-specific information" or "difficult to reuse because it depends on [supplied fact]". Never state what a real competitor, or a class of employers ("most utilities", "few employers"), says, offers or could claim.

### Test 2 — CATEGORY UBIQUITY
*Is the statement built from stock category language?*

This test is judged **against the cliché lexicon in section 4**, which is a methodology reference list. It is a statement about the text, not about how often employers use it. The auditor has no market data and never claims frequency ("most employers say this", "rare on careers pages", "few competitors could say this").

Check the claim's words against the lexicon, then its *structure*: "We invest in our people's growth" contains no single lexicon word but follows a lexicon construction ("invest in our people").
- **Rare** — no lexicon terms or constructions.
- **Common** — some lexicon terms, used together with specific content.
- **Ubiquitous** — the claim is built mainly from lexicon terms or constructions.

Phrase the finding about the text: "built from lexicon phrases ('grow with us', 'great people')", "uses no stock category vocabulary".

### Test 3 — SPECIFICITY
*Does it contain meaningful employer-specific information?*

Specificity markers (count those present in or directly attached to the claim):
- a number with scope or timeframe;
- a named practice, programme, ritual or policy with content;
- a place, customer, product or condition that only this employer has;
- a described mechanism (how something happens);
- a constraint or cost the employer accepts;
- an explicit audience or trade-off.

- **High** — two or more meaningful markers.
- **Some** — one marker, or markers present but not meaningful to candidates.
- **None** — no markers.

### Test 4 — DEFENSIBILITY
*Can this employer credibly own and support the claim?*

- **Owned** — the claim rests on something structural or costly (where the work happens, what is at stake, a practice that requires investment) and the material contains proof.
- **Supportable** — the claim is plausible for this employer and partly supported, but others could also support it.
- **Unowned** — nothing in the material ties the claim to this employer; any employer could make it with no cost.

## 2. Verdict mapping

| Substitution | Ubiquity | Specificity | Defensibility | Verdict | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Breaks | Rare / Common | High | Owned | **DISTINCTIVE** | LOW |
| Breaks or Bends | any | High or Some | Owned or Supportable | **PARTIALLY DIFFERENTIATED** | MEDIUM |
| Bends | Common | Some | Supportable | **PARTIALLY DIFFERENTIATED** | MEDIUM |
| Survives | Common / Ubiquitous | Some | Supportable / Unowned | **GENERIC** | MEDIUM–HIGH |
| Survives | Ubiquitous | None | Unowned | **GENERIC** | HIGH |

Rules:
- **Substitution is decisive.** A claim that survives substitution cannot be DISTINCTIVE, however well-worded.
- **Specific but irrelevant is not distinctive.** "Founded in 1987 in a converted mill" breaks substitution but means nothing to a candidate's decision. Verdict PARTIALLY DIFFERENTIATED at most, with a note that the specificity needs a candidate implication.
- **Distinctive but unproven.** If a claim breaks substitution but the material offers no proof, the verdict can still be DISTINCTIVE or PARTIALLY DIFFERENTIATED; the proof gap is recorded in the ledger, not hidden in the genericity verdict. Keep the two diagnostics separate.
- **When between two verdicts, choose the lower** and say what would move it up.

## 3. Output format

For each tested claim (CLAIM is the complete claim sentence quoted, because its wording is what is tested — or a close paraphrase; never a fragment that drops a time, condition, location or scope anchor; `evidence-rules.md` §10.3b):

```
CLAIM:
"Work with talented people on challenging projects."

VERDICT:
GENERIC

GENERICITY RISK:
HIGH

WHY:
The statement contains no employer-specific information, so a comparable employer could plausibly reuse it unchanged.
Substitution: survives. Ubiquity: ubiquitous ("talented people", "challenging projects"). Specificity: none. Defensibility: unowned.

WHAT WOULD MAKE IT OWNABLE:
Name the projects or problem type, and attach evidence of who the people are or how they work.
EVIDENCE NEEDED: examples of current projects; team composition or practice that makes the "talented people" claim concrete.
```

In compact contexts (content review, follow-ups) a table is acceptable:

| Claim | Substitution | Ubiquity | Specificity | Defensibility | Verdict | Risk | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

The **WHY** is mandatory. A verdict without the reason is not a finding.

## 4. Cliché lexicon

The presence of a lexicon term is a signal, not a verdict. A cliché word followed by specific proof can still be part of a differentiated claim.

**Growth and development:** grow with us, unlimited growth, career growth, endless opportunities, develop your potential, world-class training, invest in our people, learning culture, reach your full potential.

**Innovation and technology:** innovative, cutting-edge, pushing boundaries, disruptive, at the forefront, next-generation, future-ready, digital transformation, state-of-the-art.

**People and culture:** great people, talented team, the best and brightest, passionate people, like a family, supportive, collaborative, open culture, inclusive, dynamic, agile, ambitious, work hard play hard, flat hierarchy, we care.

**Impact and purpose:** make an impact, make a difference, change the world, meaningful work, shape the future, purpose-driven, be part of something bigger.

**Work and challenge:** challenging projects, exciting opportunity, fast-paced environment, every day is different, never a dull moment, ownership, entrepreneurial spirit.

**Employer status:** leading, fast-growing, best place to work, employer of choice, world-class, #1, award-winning (without the award).

**Reward:** competitive salary, attractive package, great benefits.

**Russian equivalents:** развивайся вместе с нами, безграничные возможности роста, дружная команда, мы как семья, динамично развивающаяся компания, инновационная компания, лидер рынка, интересные задачи, амбициозные проекты, конкурентная заработная плата, комфортный офис, молодой коллектив, профессиональный рост, возможность влиять на результат.

## 5. Common patterns and how to read them

| Pattern | Typical verdict | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Adjective string ("innovative, open, dynamic") | GENERIC, HIGH | Also RF07 |
| Mission sentence as EVP ("Making finance accessible to everyone") | Often PARTIALLY DIFFERENTIATED as a mission; tested separately as an EVP | The mission may be specific; the employee exchange is missing (RF03) |
| Superlative without proof ("best place to grow") | GENERIC, HIGH | Also RF02 |
| Specific number with candidate implication ("rosters fixed six weeks ahead") | DISTINCTIVE or PARTIALLY DIFFERENTIATED | Specific and relevant to schedule-sensitive candidates |
| Honest trade-off ("weekend call-outs twice a month, paid") | DISTINCTIVE or PARTIALLY DIFFERENTIATED | A stated cost deters some candidates, so it cannot be copied without accepting the same cost |
| Product scale without employee meaning ("12 million users") | PARTIALLY DIFFERENTIATED at best | Also RF22 — needs "which means you…" |

## 6. Rewriting from GENERIC towards DISTINCTIVE

The ownability ladder, applied only with facts the material contains:

1. **Generic claim** — "We invest in your growth."
2. **Mechanism** — how it happens here (named programme, practice, policy).
3. **Proof** — evidence that it happens (number, scope, attributable story).
4. **Consequence for the candidate** — what it means for someone in this role.
5. **Trade-off (optional)** — what this choice costs or who it is not for.

If a rung has no supporting material, write `EVIDENCE NEEDED:` for that rung and stop climbing. Do not invent the rung.

**The lazy-rewrite trap.** Swapping one category phrase for another ("dynamic" → "fast-moving", "great people" → "brilliant minds") does not reduce genericity. A rewrite that does not pass Test 1 has not fixed the problem; say so rather than presenting it as a fix.

## 7. Worked examples

**Example A**
CLAIM: "Shape tomorrow with the brightest minds in the sector."
VERDICT: GENERIC — RISK: HIGH
WHY: Survives substitution for virtually any employer; three lexicon constructions ("shape the future", "the best and brightest", sector superlative); no specificity markers; nothing ties it to a particular employer. Also RF02 (unsupported superlative "the brightest") and RF14/RF15-adjacent vagueness about what growth or change means.

**Example B**
CLAIM: "Rosters are fixed six weeks ahead, which lets you arrange childcare and evening study."
VERDICT: PARTIALLY DIFFERENTIATED — RISK: LOW–MEDIUM
WHY: Bends under substitution (another operator could adopt and publish the same practice, but it would have to actually do it); no lexicon terms; high specificity (a number and a candidate consequence); supportable but not structurally owned.

**Example C**
CLAIM: "Drive the night trains that get the valley's hospital staff home after late shifts."
VERDICT: DISTINCTIVE — RISK: LOW
WHY: Breaks under substitution (depends on this operator's routes and the people who use them); no lexicon terms; specific (a service, a user group, a time); owned because the network and its passengers are structural.

**Example D**
CLAIM: "We are a family."
VERDICT: GENERIC — RISK: HIGH
WHY: Survives substitution; built from a lexicon phrase ("like a family"); no specificity; unowned. It can also read ambiguously to candidates (INFERENCE: some read it as an expectation of unbounded commitment), so it carries risk beyond genericity.
