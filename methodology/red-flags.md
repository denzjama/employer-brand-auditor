# Red Flags Library

Red flags are named, recurring failure patterns in employer-brand communication. They make findings precise, comparable and easy to act on.

## How to use red flags

1. **Materiality first.** Report a red flag only when it materially affects how a candidate understands, believes or acts on the material. One cliché in an otherwise specific page is not RF01.
2. **No mechanical firing.** Do not list every flag that technically applies. A typical full audit reports 3–7 red flags. Reporting 15 is a sign the analysis has not been prioritised.
3. **Evidence required.** Each reported flag cites the text that triggers it — a precise paraphrased reference, or a quotation when the exact wording is the problem (complete sentence or the analysed phrase only; `evidence-rules.md` §10.3a–b).
4. **Merge overlaps.** If RF02 and RF18 describe the same sentence, report the one that best explains the problem and mention the other in passing.
5. **Severity is contextual.** The "typical severity" below is a starting point; adjust to the material and say why.
6. **Red flags are not scores.** They explain scores. The scorecard and red flags must be consistent (a flagged RF10 with ET1 = 3 is an error).

Report format:

```
RF07 Culture adjective soup — HIGH
Where: Careers page, "Our culture" section.
Evidence: "We are bold, curious, collaborative, agile, open and driven."
Why it matters: Six adjectives, no behaviour. A candidate learns nothing about how decisions, feedback or collaboration actually work.
Fix: Replace the list with two or three practices that show the culture. EVIDENCE NEEDED: concrete examples of how teams make decisions or give feedback.
```

### RF01 — Generic employer claim
- **Definition:** A claim that survives the competitor substitution test unchanged and carries no employer-specific content.
- **Signals:** Genericity Test verdict GENERIC on a central claim; lexicon terms with no specificity markers.
- **Example:** "Join a team where you can make an impact."
- **Why it matters:** Generic claims give no reason to choose this employer and spend the candidate's limited attention.
- **Do not fire when:** The generic phrase is incidental and immediately followed by specific proof.
- **Typical severity:** HIGH when central; LOW when peripheral.
- **Score link:** DI1, DI2.
- **Fix direction:** Apply the ownability ladder (`genericity-test.md` §6).

### RF02 — Unsupported superlative
- **Definition:** A "best", "leading", "#1", "unrivalled", "world-class" or equivalent claim with no supporting evidence of the comparison.
- **Signals:** Superlatives or rankings with no source, scope or criteria.
- **Example:** "We are the world's best employer."
- **Why it matters:** Superlatives are the least credible claim type; unsupported, they reduce trust in the surrounding claims.
- **Do not fire when:** The material names a specific source and scope (e.g. a named award with its criteria) — then evaluate support strength instead.
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM; HIGH if central to the EVP.
- **Score link:** PR1, PR2, DI4.
- **Fix direction:** Replace with the specific underlying fact, if one exists; otherwise remove. Never "soften" to a different unsupported superlative.

### RF03 — Company mission presented as EVP
- **Definition:** A statement of what the company does for customers or the world presented as the employee value proposition.
- **Signals:** The "EVP" describes customers, products or societal goals; no statement of what the employee gets.
- **Example:** "Our mission is to make financial services accessible to everyone. This is our EVP."
- **Why it matters:** A mission may motivate some candidates, but without the employee side of the exchange it does not tell a candidate what working there offers them.
- **Do not fire when:** The mission is explicitly linked to the employee experience ("you'll build the tools that…, which means…").
- **Typical severity:** HIGH (CRITICAL if it is the only proposition in a full audit).
- **Score link:** CL1, CL2.
- **Fix direction:** Keep the mission as context; add the employee exchange — what the work involves, what people get, and proof.

### RF04 — Company-first instead of candidate-first message
- **Definition:** The material talks predominantly about the company (history, size, achievements, ambitions) rather than about the candidate's experience and benefit.
- **Signals:** Most sentences have "we/our company" as subject and describe corporate attributes.
- **Example:** "Founded in 1998, we are a leading provider of integrated solutions with offices in 14 countries."
- **Why it matters:** Candidates scan for what the job means for them; company facts without implications are skipped.
- **Do not fire when:** Company facts are followed by explicit candidate implications.
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM–HIGH.
- **Score link:** CL2, CR1.
- **Fix direction:** For each company fact, add "which means for you…" or cut it.

### RF05 — Feature without candidate benefit
- **Definition:** A feature (programme, policy, tool, office, benefit) is listed without explaining why it matters to the candidate.
- **Signals:** Nouns without consequences: "Hybrid policy", "Academy", "Agile teams".
- **Example:** "We have an internal academy."
- **Why it matters:** The candidate has to guess the value; many will not.
- **Do not fire when:** The benefit is self-evident to the target audience (e.g. "25 days' holiday").
- **Typical severity:** LOW–MEDIUM.
- **Score link:** CL2, CR3.
- **Fix direction:** Add what it lets the candidate do, using facts from the material; otherwise EVIDENCE NEEDED.

### RF06 — Benefit without proof
- **Definition:** A candidate benefit is promised but the material gives no evidence that it is delivered.
- **Signals:** Ledger status UNSUPPORTED or WEAKLY SUPPORTED on a material benefit claim.
- **Example:** "Flexible working for everyone" with no description of what flexibility means in practice.
- **Why it matters:** Unproven benefits are discounted by candidates and can create mismatch if they do not hold.
- **Do not fire when:** The "benefit" is a precisely stated hygiene fact ("25 days' holiday") — that is candidate information, not a claim that needs proof (evidence-rules §1.2).
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM; HIGH for central EVP pillars.
- **Score link:** PR1, PR4.
- **Fix direction:** Attach evidence next to the claim or remove the claim. Say → Support.

### RF07 — Culture adjective soup
- **Definition:** Culture described through a list of adjectives with no behaviour, practices or examples.
- **Signals:** Three or more culture adjectives in sequence; "Our culture" sections without verbs describing what people do.
- **Example:** "We are innovative, ambitious, open and dynamic."
- **Why it matters:** Adjectives are unverifiable and interchangeable; they tell candidates nothing about how work feels.
- **Do not fire when:** Each adjective is immediately demonstrated by a practice.
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM–HIGH.
- **Score link:** ET4, DI2.
- **Fix direction:** Replace adjectives with two or three observable practices.

### RF08 — Audience blur
- **Definition:** It is unclear which candidates the material is for.
- **Signals:** "Whoever you are", "everyone", no role families named, content that mixes graduate, executive and operational messages without distinction.
- **Example:** "Wherever you are in your career, there's a seat for you at our table."
- **Why it matters:** Material written for everyone is relevant to no one in particular; target candidates cannot self-identify.
- **Do not fire when:** An inclusive statement accompanies clearly targeted content.
- **Typical severity:** HIGH in a full audit; MEDIUM in a single asset.
- **Score link:** AF1, AF2.
- **Fix direction:** Name the priority audience(s) and their decision factors.

### RF09 — One-size-fits-all EVP
- **Definition:** Materially different audiences receive identical messaging where their decision factors clearly differ.
- **Signals:** Same proposition and proof for, e.g., software engineers and warehouse staff; no adaptation by segment.
- **Example:** The same "innovative digital culture" message on both the engineering and the branch-staff careers pages.
- **Why it matters:** The proposition fits one segment at best and misrepresents the experience for others.
- **Do not fire when:** There is a shared core with segment-specific proof — that is the desired structure, not a flag.
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM–HIGH.
- **Score link:** AF3.
- **Fix direction:** Keep one core idea; adapt proof, work reality and decision factors per segment.

### RF10 — Employee voice absent
- **Definition:** No employee perspective appears in material where it would be expected (careers site, campaign, full EB set).
- **Signals:** Only corporate narration; no quotes, stories, or attributable accounts.
- **Example:** A careers site with values, perks and vacancies but no employee speaking.
- **Why it matters:** Corporate narration alone gives candidates no independent perspective on what working there is like.
- **Do not fire when:** The material type would not normally carry employee voice (single EVP line, short social ad).
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM.
- **Score link:** ET1.
- **Fix direction:** Add attributable, specific employee accounts. EVIDENCE NEEDED: real employee input — do not draft fictional quotes.

### RF11 — Work reality absent
- **Definition:** The material does not show what the work involves day to day.
- **Signals:** Aspirational descriptions only; no tasks, tools, schedules, team set-up, conditions.
- **Example:** "Be part of an exciting journey" as the description of an operations role.
- **Why it matters:** Candidates cannot assess fit; mismatch and early attrition risk rises (INFERENCE).
- **Do not fire when:** The asset is a top-of-funnel awareness piece and work reality is clearly provided one click away (verify that it is).
- **Typical severity:** HIGH for job ads and careers pages.
- **Score link:** ET2, CR2.
- **Fix direction:** Describe a real week, real tasks, real conditions using material facts.

### RF12 — Contradictory promise
- **Definition:** Two touchpoints, or two parts of one touchpoint, make incompatible statements about the employee experience.
- **Signals:** Ledger status CONFLICTING; e.g. "autonomous teams" vs. "all decisions require approval"; "work from anywhere" vs. "on site every day".
- **Example:** EVP: "Teams decide." Job ad: "Every roadmap change requires sign-off from three governance bodies."
- **Why it matters:** Contradictions are noticed by the most informed candidates and by new hires, and damage credibility of the whole proposition.
- **Do not fire when:** The difference is explained (e.g. autonomy within a stated regulatory framework).
- **Typical severity:** CRITICAL when it concerns the core promise; HIGH otherwise.
- **Score link:** CO1, CO4, PR (CONFLICTING).
- **Fix direction:** Determine which statement reflects reality (do not assume — ask or flag), then align the proposition to reality, or explain the boundary honestly.

### RF13 — Empty scale claim
- **Definition:** A claim of size or scale without a measure, or with a measure that carries no employee meaning.
- **Signals:** "Global", "massive scale", "millions", "huge impact" without numbers; numbers without "which means you…".
- **Example:** "Work at massive scale."
- **Why it matters:** Quantified scale tied to the role can be ownable; left empty, it becomes another cliché.
- **Do not fire when:** Scale is quantified and linked to the role.
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM.
- **Score link:** DI3, PR2.
- **Fix direction:** Quantify from the material and connect to the role; otherwise EVIDENCE NEEDED: scale metric.

### RF14 — Vague development promise
- **Definition:** Promises of growth, learning or career development with no mechanism, path or proof.
- **Signals:** "Unlimited growth", "career opportunities", "we invest in your development" without programmes, paths or outcomes.
- **Example:** "We offer unprecedented career growth."
- **Why it matters:** Development is a likely decision factor for many roles (INFERENCE from the role), and a promise with no mechanism gives candidates nothing to check, so it is easy to discount.
- **Do not fire when:** A mechanism and some outcome evidence are present.
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM–HIGH.
- **Score link:** PR1, DI2, CR3.
- **Fix direction:** Mechanism + proof + path. EVIDENCE NEEDED: internal promotion data, programme content, typical progression routes.

### RF15 — Innovation cliché
- **Definition:** Claims of innovation, cutting-edge technology or disruption without showing what is new or how candidates engage with it.
- **Signals:** "Innovative", "cutting-edge", "pushing boundaries" without technologies, problems or examples.
- **Example:** "Our engineers live at the frontier of next-generation tech."
- **Why it matters:** Technical and creative candidates in particular look for concrete problems and tools; abstract innovation language reads as filler (INFERENCE).
- **Do not fire when:** Specific technologies, problems or experiments are named.
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM.
- **Score link:** DI2, ET2.
- **Fix direction:** Name the problem, the tools, and the candidate's role in it.

### RF16 — Great-team cliché
- **Definition:** Claims about the quality of colleagues ("the best people", "talented team", "like a family") without evidence.
- **Signals:** Team-quality claims with no description of who the people are, how they work, or what makes them notable.
- **Example:** "Join a team of exceptional minds."
- **Why it matters:** The claim is plausibly reusable by any comparable employer because it contains no information about who the colleagues are or how they work.
- **Do not fire when:** Team composition, practices or achievements are shown.
- **Typical severity:** LOW–MEDIUM.
- **Score link:** DI2, PR1.
- **Fix direction:** Show how the team works, or remove.

### RF17 — Perk dumping
- **Definition:** A long list of perks presented as the reason to join, without hierarchy, relevance or link to a proposition.
- **Signals:** 8+ benefits in a flat list; trivial perks (fruit, table tennis) next to material ones (pension, leave) without distinction.
- **Example:** "Competitive salary, fruit, ping-pong, birthday off, casual Fridays, team building, snacks, modern office."
- **Why it matters:** Perks are features, not a proposition; flat lists bury the benefits that matter and make the employer look interchangeable.
- **Do not fire when:** A complete benefits list is supplied as reference information beneath a clear proposition.
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM.
- **Score link:** CL3, DI2, AF2.
- **Fix direction:** Lead with the 2–3 benefits that matter to the target audience, state them precisely, move the rest to a reference list.

### RF18 — Overclaiming
- **Definition:** Claims that exceed what the material's own evidence shows, even when some evidence exists.
- **Signals:** Ledger status PARTIALLY SUPPORTED because the claim's scope outruns its evidence; "always", "everyone", "every day".
- **Example:** "Everyone gets promoted fast" supported by one employee story.
- **Why it matters:** Overclaiming converts real proof into doubt; new hires notice the gap.
- **Do not fire when:** The claim is scoped to the evidence.
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM.
- **Score link:** PR3, CO2.
- **Fix direction:** Scope the claim to what is shown.

### RF19 — Unexplained internal jargon
- **Definition:** Terms, acronyms, framework names or abstract management language that the supplied candidate-facing material does not explain, so a reader without internal context may not understand them.
- **Signals:** "Operationalise our north-star pillars", "drive cross-portfolio alignment", internal programme acronyms, team or tool names used without explanation.
- **Example:** "Drive cross-portfolio alignment on our north-star value framework."
- **Why it matters:** Unexplained terms slow comprehension and signal that the text was written for insiders. Frame the finding as a comprehension risk grounded in the material: "This term is not explained in the supplied candidate-facing material, so its meaning may be unclear to a reader without internal context." Do not claim what candidates generally know, or that a term is "standard", "industry language" or "normal vocabulary", without evidence (`evidence-rules.md` §8).
- **Do not fire when:** The material itself explains the term, or the term is the role's own name or a qualification the ad states as a requirement. If you judge that the target audience is likely to know a term, say so as an INFERENCE about comprehension, not as a fact about the market.
- **Typical severity:** LOW–MEDIUM.
- **Score link:** CL4.
- **Fix direction:** Replace with plain description of the work, or explain the term where it first appears.

### RF20 — Weak or missing CTA
- **Definition:** The candidate is not given a clear, relevant next step, or the next step is generic and unsupported.
- **Signals:** No CTA; "Send your CV" with no context; no information about what happens next.
- **Example:** "Interested? Send us your CV."
- **Why it matters:** Interested candidates drop off where the next step is unclear or effortful (INFERENCE).
- **Do not fire when:** The asset is not meant to drive action (an internal strategy document).
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM.
- **Score link:** CV1–CV5.
- **Fix direction:** Specific action, effort required, what happens next.

### RF21 — Values presented as differentiation without evidence
- **Definition:** Corporate values are offered as the reason to join, without showing how they shape work.
- **Signals:** "Our values: Integrity, Excellence, Respect" as the culture section; values as EVP pillars without proof.
- **Example:** "What sets us apart? Our values: Integrity, Courage, Care, Quality."
- **Why it matters:** Value words are near-universal; differentiation comes from what the values cause people to do.
- **Do not fire when:** Each value is tied to a concrete practice or decision.
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM.
- **Score link:** DI2, ET4.
- **Fix direction:** For each value kept, one practice that shows it; cut values that cannot be shown.

### RF22 — Proof without candidate relevance
- **Definition:** Evidence is present but supports something that does not matter to the candidate's decision, or its employee implication is never stated.
- **Signals:** Product, revenue or customer statistics with no "which means for you"; awards for products presented on the careers page.
- **Example:** "Our app processes 40 million transactions a month" with no explanation of what that means for an engineer's work.
- **Why it matters:** Strong numbers are wasted if the candidate has to infer their relevance.
- **Do not fire when:** The implication is explicit.
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM.
- **Score link:** PR3, CR1.
- **Fix direction:** Add the employee implication, using facts from the material; EVIDENCE NEEDED if the implication requires new facts (e.g. what engineers own).

### RF23 — Employer promise disconnected from content
- **Definition:** The EVP or tagline appears in one place but the rest of the content does not express, demonstrate or even refer to it.
- **Signals:** EVP pillars absent from job ads, stories and campaigns; content themes unrelated to the EVP.
- **Example:** EVP: "Power the valley's night shift." Job ads and stories never mention the valley, the night service or who it serves.
- **Why it matters:** A promise that only appears in a tagline does not shape perception.
- **Do not fire when:** Only one touchpoint is supplied (consistency not assessable).
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM–HIGH.
- **Score link:** CO1, CO2.
- **Fix direction:** Carry the core idea and its proof into each touchpoint.

### RF24 — Excessive polish / insufficient work reality
- **Definition:** Uniformly positive, polished material with no friction, difficulty or trade-off, so that it reads as advertising rather than information.
- **Signals:** No mention of demands, pace, constraints, downsides; every quote glowing; stock-photo tone.
- **Example:** A warehouse role described only as "exciting" and "rewarding", with no mention of shifts, physical demands or peak periods.
- **Why it matters:** Candidates trust material more when it acknowledges difficulty (INFERENCE grounded in professional practice); polish without reality also increases mismatch.
- **Do not fire when:** Some honest complexity is present, even if limited.
- **Typical severity:** MEDIUM.
- **Score link:** ET3, AF4, CR4.
- **Fix direction:** Name one or two real demands and how they are handled, using facts from the material; EVIDENCE NEEDED otherwise.
